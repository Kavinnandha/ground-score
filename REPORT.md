## 1. Problem framing

### What "good" means for this brand

@AmazonHelp is a public, high-volume, logistics-heavy support queue. Reading the
corpus, three things shape what a useful agent looks like:

**Most inbound is a status question, not a problem to solve.** "Where is my
parcel", "it says delivered and it isn't here", "I was charged twice". The
brand's own replies are usually a short apology plus one instruction or link.
An agent that produces exactly that is not being lazy — it is matching the job.

**The brand rarely hides behind DMs, but it does hand off.** 10.8% of first
replies route the customer elsewhere (0.8% to DMs, 9.9% to a contact page).
That is low enough that "draft a grounded reply" is a real task here — unlike
Uber_Support at 75% — but high enough that a system which learns to imitate the
brand will learn to hand off roughly one time in ten.

**The expensive mistake is public and permanent.** A needless escalation costs
an agent thirty seconds. A wrong auto-reply is a public tweet from a brand
account, and the cases where it goes wrong — fraud, a damaged item, someone
who has already been let down twice — are exactly the cases where the customer
is least able to absorb it.

So "good" here is:

1. **Correct routing far more than correct replies.** The system's value is
   knowing what it should not answer.
2. **Grounded over fluent.** A vague-but-true reply beats a confident invented
   one. The rubric enforces this: fabricated specifics score below honest hedging.
3. **Auditable.** A support lead must be able to ask "why did this escalate?"
   and get a rule name, not a vibe.
4. **Coverage is the business case, harm rate is the constraint.** The headline
   is therefore an operating point — *X% auto-handled at ≤Y% false-auto* — not
   an accuracy figure.

### What I deliberately did not build

- **Multi-turn dialogue.** The agent acts on the *first* customer message only.
  Later turns are kept for context and for measuring whether the brand's answer
  ended the conversation, but conversational state is out of scope.
- **Live tool use.** No order lookup, no account access. This is the single
  biggest cap on the ceiling here: a large share of these tickets are
  *unanswerable* without account data, and no amount of prompt work fixes that.
  Recognising those and escalating them is the correct behaviour, and it is what
  the system does.
- **PII redaction.** PII is *detected* and used as an escalation trigger, not
  scrubbed or stored.
- **Fine-tuning.** Retrieval plus a taxonomy gets most of the value at a
  fraction of the complexity, and it stays inspectable.
- **Multilingual support.** Non-Latin-script messages are filtered out of the
  corpus. They exist in the queue; this system does not serve them.
- **Serving.** No API, no queue consumer, no latency budget. The deliverable is
  an evaluated system, not a deployed one.

## 2. Method

| Stage | Approach |
|---|---|
| Brand choice | Profiled 7 candidate brands on handoff rate, reply substance, intent diversity, multi-turn rate (`results/brand_profile.md`) |
| Corpus | 10,026 threads, stable-hash split into 9,427 retrieval history / 599 evaluation pool |
| Taxonomy | Cluster → LLM names each cluster → human merges into `taxonomy/intents.yaml` |
| Golden set | 200 adjudicated examples in 3 strata, scored separately |
| Agent | retrieve(k=5) → classify → draft(≤280 chars, cites precedent) → route |
| Routing | Deterministic rules first; LLM judges only what survives them |
| Evaluation | Bootstrap CIs, coverage-vs-harm curve, LLM judge validated against blind human scores |

Full rationale for each choice is in [`DECISIONS.md`](DECISIONS.md).

| AppleSupport | 3949 | 0.536 | 0.035 | **0.551** | 0.394 | 0.350 | 0.130 |
| Uber_Support | 3953 | 0.648 | 0.119 | **0.750** | 0.170 | 0.357 | 0.126 |

My pre-registered guess was SpotifyCares. The data rejected it at 37.7% handoff.

**The first version of this table was wrong.** It measured only DM-style
deflection and put AmazonHelp at 0.008. Reading actual retrieved replies showed
the real pattern — AmazonHelp says *"please reach out to us here: `<URL>`"*, not
"DM us" — and correcting the metric moved it to 0.108, a 13× undercount. The
ranking survived, so the decision stands, but it stood on a number that was
wrong until it was checked against raw text. A bare URL is deliberately not
counted as a handoff: 46% of replies contain one and most are genuinely useful.

AmazonHelp over hulu_support is the one genuinely judgement-based call: hulu
wins on handoff rate and substance, but its lexical diversity (0.124) and
multi-turn rate (0.383) point to an intent space that collapses to ~4 classes,
making the classification result uninformative.

## 5. What is misleading about my headline number?

This section is mandatory in the brief and it is the one I would read first.

**1. There is one annotator, so the agreement figure is intra-annotator.**
The κ reported for label quality comes from re-labelling 50 examples blind, at
least a day apart. That measures self-consistency and is a *ceiling* on label
quality, not inter-annotator agreement. A second annotator would almost
certainly agree less. Every metric derived from these labels therefore has more
slack in it than its confidence interval suggests, because the CI accounts for
sampling noise but not for label noise.

**2. The golden set was pre-labelled by the system it evaluates.**
Weak labels came from the classifier and were adjudicated by hand. The override
rate is published for exactly this reason — the lower it is, the more the
golden set measures agreement-with-the-model rather than ground truth. This is
an anchoring bias that no amount of care fully removes.

**3. n = 120 on the test split. The intervals are wide.**
Differences under roughly 10 points between systems are not distinguishable
from noise at this sample size. Any ranking that depends on a small gap should
be read as "not established", not "smaller".

**4. The headline is one stratum, and the pooled number is not production performance.**
The golden set deliberately over-samples rare intents (25%) and hard cases
(15%). Pooling all 200 would understate real-traffic accuracy; reporting only
the proportional stratum would hide the tail. Both are given, but any single
number quoted out of this report will be misleading in one direction or the
other.

**5. Historical replies are what the brand *did*, not what was *good*.**
Retrieval grounds the agent in precedent — including precedent where the brand
handed off, apologised without acting, or answered a different question. An
agent that imitates this queue perfectly would hand off ~10% of the time. The
rubric deliberately scores deflection as a resolution failure to avoid
rewarding that, which means the reply scores are measured against a standard
the brand's own historical replies do not always meet.

**6. The escalation labels are my judgement, not Amazon's policy.**
"Should a human handle this?" was decided by me against a written guideline. A
real support organisation has staffing, SLA and liability constraints that
would move that line. The routing metrics measure agreement with my policy, not
correctness against a real one.

**7. The judge and the drafter are the same model family.**
No pro-tier quota was available on this key, so the judge is a *different
generation* of Gemini flash rather than a different tier. Self-preference is
therefore plausible and is measured, not assumed: a cross-family judge
(`gemma-4-31b-it`) re-scores a subset, and the gap is reported as an upper bound
on the inflation. The main reply-quality number is still produced by a
same-family judge.

**8. The hard-case slice was designed by the same person who built the agent.**
It uses model-independent surface heuristics precisely to limit this, but the
choice of *which* heuristics count as hard is still mine.

**9. Thresholds are fitted on ~80 dev examples.**
`tau_confidence` and `tau_similarity` are point estimates from a small sample.
They will not transfer cleanly to a new period or a new brand, and the
operating point they define is more fragile than a single reported coverage
number implies.

**10. The confidence score is not calibrated.**
Routing partly depends on a self-reported LLM confidence, which is known to
cluster high and behave more like a fluency signal than a probability. It is
used because the alternative — no confidence signal — is worse, not because it
is trustworthy.

**11. One brand, one snapshot.**
Everything here is AmazonHelp in late 2017. Nothing establishes that the
taxonomy, the thresholds, or the judge's behaviour transfers to another brand
or another year.

## 6. What I would do with one more week

Ranked by how much they would change what I currently believe:

1. **A second annotator on 100 examples.** Real inter-annotator κ would replace
   the weakest claim in the report with a measured one. Highest value by far.
2. **Human preference over pairwise comparisons.** Absolute 1–5 judge scores are
   the least reliable part of the reply evaluation; pairwise A/B against the
   real historical reply would be more robust and directly answers "is this
   better than what the brand actually sent?".
3. **Calibrate the confidence signal.** Fit a reliability curve on dev, then
   route on calibrated probability rather than raw self-report — the routing
   thresholds are currently sitting on an uncalibrated number.
4. **Transfer test on a second brand.** Re-run the whole pipeline on
   hulu_support without re-tuning. Whatever degrades is the part that was
   overfitted to AmazonHelp.
5. **Cost and latency accounting.** Three LLM calls per message is a real
   operating cost that nothing here measures; a cheaper router (rules-only for
   the easy majority) is likely most of the value.
6. **Retrieval quality ablation.** Vary k, compare embedding backends, and
   measure how much reply quality actually depends on retrieval depth.
